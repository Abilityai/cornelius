# Letter to SoTA - New Defense   
*[Sign up to hack here](http://lu.ma/edth-2025-london) *   
Dear SoTA,   
I'm writing to invite you to London Defense Tech Week and the European Defense Tech Hackathon in London, where we're tackling Europe's most pressing defense challenges.   
   
This past Sunday was Ukraine's Independence Day, and the number of effective deep strikes into Russia has been increasing since June 01 with Operation Spider Web. On the same day, Ukrainian military struck a transformer which was part of a nuclear power plant in Kursk, Russia, halving it's power output. Ukraine is able to fight back as effectively because of their rate of innovation. Our mission at the *[European Defense Tech Hub](http://eurodefense.tech)* is to bring this rate of innovation to the rest of Europe before it's too late. On September 25-28 we are hosting our 13th defense tech-focused hackathon with SoTA in London.    
   
If there's one thing I've learned in the last year of since we started the European Defense Tech Hub, it's that every couple of months, there's another "wake-up call" for the public, for politicians, militaries and for investors. It's hard to believe, but a bit over a year ago when I was reaching out to student associations of some of the top technical universities in Europe to help get the best talent to work on the most pressing problems today, the response was that of moralistic outrage. The vibe shifted within months, in fact, I like to think that our first event in June last year, where we gathered Ukrainian teams, members of the MoD, Foreign legion fighters, and 150 talented and driven engineers, was part of making it shift. A few months later, we hosted the next couple of events, in Copenhagen and Paris, and then again around the Munich Security Conference, where JD Vance shook the Europeans awake, making it so blatantly obvious as only an American can, that Europe is on its own in the fight for our democracy. Since then, we've accelerated and are pushing even harder to be the network for defense tech innovation in Europe. We continue to get wake-up calls around the urgency of solving these problems, and are responding weekly.   
   
The wave of innovation seen in Ukraine has not only been driven by new technology. DJI had been making drones for years, and in fact, there were Ukrainians using them in Crimea since 2014. The catalyst was adaptation: taking civilian drones, analyzing their capabilities, and developing new military tactics around them. What keeps the wave of innovation going, is the constant changing of the requirements. The paradigm of warfare has been revolutionized since a few years now, because of the effectiveness of unmanned systems. Due to these changing requirements and constant cat-and-mouse game of tech development in Ukraine, how military units operate has had to change as well. To quote a Ukranian soldier part of a brigade we work with: "No drone we get delivered, whether Ukrainian or foreign, is ready to be used out of the box. They all need re-engineering." This means that engineers work alongside military units in the field, close to the frontline to gather data from soldiers, adapt the equipment they have, whether that's changing frequency hopping algorithm on an ISR drone, or if it's welding new rails onto an unmanned ground vehicle.   
   
The technological challenges that Europe faces aren't all directly related to the use of flying unmanned, attritable systems like the kamikaze drones we see in the media. As part of our hackathons, we talk to frontline operators, military contractors, VCs, staff of various MoDs, and defense tech startups like Helsing, Comand, STARK, Nordic Air Defence, and more in order to provide enough context for a talented team to build a new startup straight out of the hackathon. The topics range from navigation in GNSS-denied environments, image intelligence, techniques for tackling (or spreading) misinformation, acoustic detection of fiber-optic drones, coupling radios with LLMs for reports and intelligence gather, and much much more.    
   
Here are some thoughts on what could be generally interesting topics that could lead to national strategic advantages in a hybrid or direct warfare situation:   
- AI-directed diplomacy for avoiding conflict, i.e., hyper personalized and exclusive AI Chief of Staff for high ranking officials in foreign policy    
- Manipulating the energy generation and supply capabilities of specific geographies of military significance to still supply civilian population but not allow for military advances   
- Procurement policy and effectiveness of public money for optimal protection   
    - Use LLMs to source, write proposals and win contracts (private side)   
    - Platforms for coordinating group submissions public processes (private side)   
    - Using LLMs to review proposals (public side)   
    - Using technology to speed up requirements setting and communication for new acquisitions (public side)   
- Designing a custom board for running automous operations (instead of e.g., RPi or NVIDIA Jetsons) at low cost   
- How to provide and deliver large amounts of compute and connectivity to an area of operations without internet or grid connectivity, e.g., rapidly setting up secure local networks, with ability to be connected to one or more relays with external connectivity (e.g., to HQ)   
   
   
The list goes on and on. If any of these topics entice you, please consider attending our London Defense Tech hackathon, opening the London Defense Tech Week in September. We'll have our partners representing interesting problem sets, like one-way effectors (STARK), ISR drones (Quantum Systems, UGVs (Arx), general autonomy (Delian), communcations and security (eyeson) and if you're just generally into high-performing databases, then our friends at SurrealDB will be around to help out. We'll also ensure to have military experts both from the UK and Ukraine militaries.   
   
Thank you, and see you on the 25th of September in London!   
Jonatan
