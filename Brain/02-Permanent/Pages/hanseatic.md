# Hanseatic   
1. Macro factors are lining up to **increase volatility in energy prices massively**   
    1. Weather is growing more volatile, which impacts energy supply anddemand simultaneously   
    2. [Electrification is accelerating](https://lowcarbonpower.org/changes-electrification), but unevenly across regions (→ arbitrage opportunities)   
    3. European energy markets just introduced a [15-min power trading window](https://flex-power.energy/news/first-day-ahead-trading-15-min/)   
    4. All other parts of the [Electric slide](https://electricslide.notboring.com/) are moving   
2. Incumbent energy traders have no pressure to move, and customers are unhappy   
    1. Big physical energy traders rely on scale, they have massive amounts of working capital, and make $B profits on margins. E.g., Mercuria made almost $3B in profits 2024, they started a new metals trading team this year that made $300M profits → no reason to go after opportunities that require a lot of work   
    2. Customers value reliability over perfect pricing, and higher volatility puts higher demand for expertise on in house energyprocurers; expertise most utilities don't even have   
    3. Emerging Southeast Europe region is underserved with modern energy traders, yet booming industrial regions (customers) as Europe re-industrializes   
3. Next-gen Energy trader will look like a combination of Stripe, Tesla and Amazon:   
    1. API-first, easy to integrate for customers and dealing with operational headaches out of the box (e.g., import papers, licenses)   
    2. Fully programmatic, integrated back-office operations, risk management and pricing predictions   
    3. Integrated logistics for physical delivery, quality assurance, etc.    
4. Things that make it look strange   
    1. Most other VCs don't know the market, rather look downstream from consumption, or at generation only (e.g., fusion)   
    2. Network effects are there but different: Hanseatic benefits from *network density* by having more consumers in one area allows them to re-route a shipment easily in case a customer can't accept it, and if a customer asks for another product, Hanseatic benefits from supplier density by being able to sell more things to customers → drives trust and customer connection   
    3. It smells like a fund-of-fund investment, but it's really not. It's like Amazon in the sense that they buy and sell physical goods (gas, electrons, oil, metals) and like Revolut in the sense that they make money offering better interfaces and user experiences than incumbents, while automating the backend   
    4. Unclear exit path: acquisitions are on book value and customer contracts, not tech multiples, might be dividends involved. They floated the idea of the option to trigger a buy-out at some point at market value.   
    5. Other risks than usual: mostly financial risks, regulatory, less tech risk   
    6. Funding will be some equity (1.6M now, 10M next year) and then profitable and only in need of working capital (ca 50M to begin with)
