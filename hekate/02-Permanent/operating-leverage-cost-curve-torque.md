# Operating Leverage Is the Cost Curve: Worst-Margin Producers Have the Most Torque

In a commodity bull run, the best-performing producer equities are usually the ones with the *worst* margins - the highest-cost operators sitting at the top of the global cost curve. The mechanism is operating leverage stated in cost-curve terms.

A producer's gross profit per unit is `Price - Cash Cost`. The percentage change in that profit for a given price move is:

```
earnings torque = Price / (Price - Cost)
```

The denominator is the margin. A thin margin (high cost) means any price move is a large percentage swing in profit. A fat margin (low cost) dilutes the same move. So the cost curve *is* a leverage ladder: the higher a producer sits on it, the more its earnings - and its equity - swing with the commodity price.

The function is **convex and bidirectional**. At high prices everyone has fat margins and the torque spread is benign. As the price falls toward the high-cost cohort's all-in cost, their torque multiple goes vertical and then negative - they hit their cost wall first, free cash flow collapses non-linearly, and the equity reprices toward distress while low-cost anchors merely see compressed margins. This is why the worst-margin names are best in a bull run *and* worst in a flat/bear market: it is the same convexity read in two directions.

The practical consequence: the highest-cost producers are a **leveraged bet on the commodity's direction**, not a structural hold. Own them when bullish, size them as the high-beta sleeve, and rotate to low-cost anchors when neutral-to-bearish. A long-low-cost / short-high-cost pair harvests the convexity directly when you expect the price to go sideways or down.
